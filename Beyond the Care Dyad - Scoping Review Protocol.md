# Beyond the Care Dyad: Bridging Dementia Caregivers with eHealth: Are (Some of) the Bridges Still Too Far? 
## eHealth Applications to Support Communication, Coordination, Collaboration Among Caregivers of People with Dementia Living at Home - A Scoping Review Protocol

## Abstract

**Background:** Supporting persons with dementia living at home often relies upon complex care networks extending beyond the primary caregiver-person with dementia dyad. Effective communication, coordination, and collaboration (CCC) within these networks are considered fundamental for optimal care and caregiver well-being, yet encounter considerable barriers. Electronic health (eHealth) technologies present potential pathways, conceptualized as digital "bridges," to support these networks. However, the development, evaluation, implementation, and sustainability of such technologies appear challenging. The evidence base remains fragmented, a unified terminology is lacking, and a significant gap persists between research findings and the adoption of these tools into routine practice, often termed the "pilot-to-practice chasm" or "valley of death".

**Objective:** This scoping review protocol delineates a study designed to systematically map the landscape of eHealth applications conceived as digital "bridges" to support CCC and social or psychosocial support among diverse caregivers (formal and informal) within dementia home care networks. Specifically, it aims to identify the types of bridges developed, the individuals they connect, the processes and features involved in their development and evaluation, their reported effectiveness, stakeholder perspectives, evolution, sustainability, and the factors influencing implementation – thereby addressing why certain bridges may remain difficult to implement successfully or sustain in practice ("too far").

**Methods:** Following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, systematic searches will be conducted in PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, ACM Digital Library, and IEEE Xplore (from January 2000 onwards). Searches of grey literature sources (e.g., app stores, targeted web searches, organizational websites) and citation searching will supplement database inquiries. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts and full texts against predefined eligibility criteria based on the Population, Concept, Context framework. Data will be extracted using a structured charting form covering source details, bridge type and features, development methodology, evaluation approach and outcomes, stakeholder views, evolution/sustainability, and implementation factors. Findings will be mapped using a narrative synthesis structured around the review questions and the "bridges" metaphor.

**Discussion:** This protocol outlines the methodology for a scoping review anticipated to provide a comprehensive overview of existing eHealth bridges for dementia caregiver networks. The findings are intended to illuminate the types, features, evaluation approaches, effectiveness evidence, critical implementation challenges, and research gaps. This synthesis aims to inform future research endeavours, technology development directed towards creating "better bridges that last," clinical practice, and policy initiatives focused on enhancing support for collaborative dementia care within the home setting.

**Keywords:** Dementia, Alzheimer Disease, Caregivers, Informal Caregivers, Family Caregivers, Caregiving Network, eHealth, Mobile Health, Technology, Communication, Coordination, Collaboration, Social Support, Psychosocial Support, Implementation Science, Scoping Review, Protocol.

## 1. Introduction

Dementia constitutes a substantial and escalating global health concern, marked by progressive cognitive deterioration that profoundly impacts individuals' functional capacity and quality of life. The worldwide prevalence of dementia is considerable and projected to increase significantly, particularly within aging populations, thereby imposing immense strain upon health and social care systems. Consequently, enabling persons with dementia (PwD) to reside in their own homes for as long as feasible frequently necessitates complex care arrangements.

These arrangements typically involve intricate networks of individuals, extending well beyond the traditionally examined primary caregiver-person with dementia dyad. Such networks commonly encompass primary informal caregivers (e.g., spouses, adult children), secondary informal caregivers (e.g., other relatives, friends, neighbours), alongside various formal care providers (e.g., home care professionals, community nurses, general practitioners, medical specialists). The collective effort of this diverse group is often essential for maintaining the person with dementia at home (Dam et al., 2017).

The effective functioning of such a care network is fundamentally contingent upon adequate communication, coordination, and collaboration (CCC) among its diverse members. Effective teamwork facilitates shared understanding, appropriate distribution of tasks, timely information exchange, and ultimately, more person-centred and integrated care. However, achieving seamless CCC within these often dynamic and geographically dispersed networks encounters numerous obstacles. These include challenges in sharing relevant information appropriately and securely, significant variability in dementia-related knowledge and health literacy across network members, the considerable emotional sequelae of caregiving and complex interpersonal dynamics, as well as systemic impediments within fragmented healthcare and social support systems (Dam et al., 2016).

Electronic health (eHealth) technologies, defined broadly as health services and information delivered or enhanced through the Internet and related technologies, encompassing a wide range of tools from web-based platforms and mobile applications to potentially more advanced systems incorporating artificial intelligence (AI), may offer pathways—conceptualized metaphorically in this review as digital "bridges"—to help address these barriers. These digital bridges could potentially facilitate more efficient and effective information exchange, support the coordination of complex care tasks, promote shared decision-making, and foster a sense of shared understanding and mutual support within the care network. Such bridges might encompass various forms, ranging from dedicated care coordination platforms focusing on task management and scheduling, to care collaboration platforms supporting joint care planning and decision-making, knowledge and education systems aimed at improving dementia literacy, communication and accompanying support systems (e.g., facilitating interaction with the person with dementia or among caregivers), psychosocial support tools specifically for caregivers, location tracking and wandering support systems for safety, or even comprehensive care management and planning systems integrating multiple functionalities (Christie et al., 2022).

Despite this considerable potential, and evidence from some studies indicating positive effects of certain eHealth interventions, often assessed within controlled research contexts (Dam et al., 2019), a notable and persistent gap exists between research findings and the successful implementation and sustained use of these technologies in everyday dementia care practice. Many eHealth applications developed may lack features specifically tailored for multi-caregiver network support, focusing instead on the individual caregiver or the dyad. Others face significant usability challenges, particularly for older users or those with limited digital skills, prove difficult to integrate into existing care practices and workflows, or encounter insurmountable barriers related to costs, lack of trust, and privacy concerns. This phenomenon, where promising interventions fail to transition into practice, is often referred to as the "pilot-to-practice chasm" or the "valley of death" (Christie et al., 2018).

Furthermore, the academic literature does not employ consistent terminology for technologies aimed at supporting "care collaboration" or network-based interactions, using a variety of overlapping terms which complicates efforts to systematically identify, synthesize, and compare the available evidence. This lack of conceptual clarity is particularly problematic given the heterogeneous mix of interventions available: academically developed prototypes, often evaluated primarily for feasibility or usability in small-scale studies (Dam et al., 2017), and commercially available applications, which frequently lack rigorous independent assessment of their effectiveness or impact on network collaboration. Moreover, understanding how these digital bridges have been developed (e.g., user involvement through co-design, theoretical basis), how they have been evaluated methodologically (e.g., study designs, outcome measures), their evolution over time, and whether they remain operational ("still standing") is crucial for assessing the field's progress and identifying potentially sustainable solutions (Christie et al., 2020).

The critical question arises whether, despite their theoretical potential, some of these technological bridges remain challenging to implement successfully and sustain in practice, thus being effectively "too far" for widespread practical application in the complex reality of dementia home care networks. This review aims to map this landscape, considering not only the existence and effectiveness of these bridges but also the crucial factors influencing their implementation and sustainability.

Therefore, a scoping review is deemed necessary to systematically map this complex and fragmented area. Scoping reviews are particularly suitable for clarifying key concepts, examining the extent, range, and nature of evidence in heterogeneous fields, identifying research gaps, and summarizing findings from diverse bodies of literature, following established methodologies. This review aims to identify and characterize the range of eHealth applications developed as digital "bridges" intended to facilitate CCC and provide social or psychosocial support among the diverse members of dementia care networks, primarily within the home-care setting. The review will examine the characteristics of these bridges (what types exist, how they were built – encompassing both development process and structural features), the network members they target (who are the bridges built for?), the available evidence regarding their utilization, effectiveness, and crucially, how they have been evaluated. It will also explore the needs these bridges aim to fulfill, the barriers hindering their successful adoption and sustained use (investigating why some bridges might remain difficult to implement or sustain), their evolution over time, and their sustainability in practice (Bartels et al., 2021).

### 1.1. Background and Rationale

#### 1.1.1. The Inherently Networked Nature of Dementia Home Care: More Than Just a Dyad

Providing care for a person with dementia within their home environment is seldom the responsibility of a single individual. Rather, it typically depends upon a complex and often dynamic network involving multiple persons. This network frequently includes a primary informal caregiver, often a spouse or adult child who assumes the main responsibility, alongside secondary informal caregivers such as other family members, friends, and neighbours who may provide supplementary support or respite. Formal home care providers (e.g., personal care assistants, home health aides), community support personnel (e.g., case managers, day centre staff), and various healthcare professionals (e.g., general practitioners, community nurses, specialists) are also integral components of this care ecosystem. The collective input from this diverse group provides essential practical assistance (e.g., help with activities of daily living, medication management), emotional support, and different perspectives considered crucial for delivering holistic, person-centred care that adapts to the changing needs associated with dementia progression (Dam et al., 2017).

Research increasingly recognizes the significant contributions of individuals beyond the traditionally focused primary caregiver-person with dementia dyad. Nonetheless, a significant focus in both research and intervention development often remains centred on this dyad. This persistent dyadic focus may inadvertently lead to an underappreciation of the roles, contributions, stresses, and distinct needs experienced by other members of the care network. It is not unusual, particularly as care demands intensify with the progression of dementia, for multiple individuals to share primary caregiving tasks or for secondary caregivers to play substantial roles. These care networks vary considerably in size, composition, and dynamics, often expanding or shifting as the person with dementia requires increased assistance with medical care, mobility, personal care, or supervision (Dam et al., 2017).

Caregivers within the network undertake numerous functions. Beyond assisting with daily activities, they often serve as vital communicators, mediators, and advocates, bridging the person with dementia and the wider social and healthcare environment. The involvement of the entire care network—encompassing the person with dementia, various caregivers (formal and informal), and relevant healthcare professionals—is considered fundamental for achieving truly integrated and person-centred care. Such comprehensive involvement helps ensure that care planning addresses not only clinical aspects but also the holistic well-being, personal values, life history, and social context pertinent to the person with dementia and their family. As dementia progresses and individuals may experience increasing difficulty in articulating their own needs and preferences, input from family members and other involved caregivers becomes especially valuable for ensuring that care remains aligned with the person's known wishes and identity.

However, the contributions and needs of this wider network may be rendered less visible by research paradigms and support systems that have historically concentrated primarily on the primary caregiver. This narrow focus can result in the development of interventions, including eHealth tools, that do not adequately support the complex, multi-directional communication and coordination required within real-world care networks. The persistent focus on the primary caregiver-person with dementia dyad in much research and intervention design, possibly driven by the methodological complexities of studying dynamic networks compared to simpler dyads, may inadvertently obscure the complex realities of care networks. This potentially hinders the development of eHealth tools that effectively support the multi-directional communication and coordination required in practice. This "invisibility" of the network likely perpetuates the design of eHealth tools that fail to support real-world network dynamics. A concerning possibility is that when such dyad-focused tools demonstrate limited utility in complex network situations, this outcome might be misinterpreted not as a failure of the tool's design focus, but as a general limitation of eHealth in complex care scenarios. Such misinterpretations could discourage further investment in developing more sophisticated, network-centric solutions, thereby reinforcing the cycle of dyadic focus. As caregivers often function as crucial communication hubs and advocates, technological solutions should ideally be designed not merely to support the primary caregiver in isolation, but rather to enhance connectivity, shared understanding, and collaborative action across the entire network. This scoping review employs the metaphor of "bridges" to signify these necessary technological and communication pathways, emphasizing the critical need to build connections that acknowledge and empower the whole network, facilitating effective teamwork. Understanding the types of bridges built, who they are intended to connect, how they function in practice, and their long-term sustainability is therefore key to improving support for networked dementia care.

#### 1.1.2. Knowledge Gaps, Misconceptions, and Varied Literacy Across the Caregiver Network: Weak Foundations for Bridge Building?

Effective communication and collaboration within any care network presuppose a foundational level of shared understanding regarding the condition being managed. In the context of dementia, however, studies consistently indicate suboptimal levels of dementia knowledge among the general public and, importantly, also among family caregivers. This knowledge deficit extends beyond general awareness to encompass specific, potentially detrimental misconceptions about the nature, progression, and management of dementia syndromes. Commonly held erroneous beliefs, for example, viewing dementia as a normal or inevitable aspect of aging, misunderstanding the communication changes associated with the condition, or misinterpreting behavioral symptoms, can impede appropriate care decisions, delay help-seeking behaviour, and negatively influence interactions with the person living with dementia. Insufficient knowledge might lead caregivers to hold unrealistic expectations regarding the person's abilities, potentially causing frustration and conflict, or leave them uncertain about the type or amount of care required and unaware of potentially available resources and support services. While eHealth bridges designed as knowledge and education systems aim to address this, their effectiveness across diverse network members with varying baseline knowledge needs systematic mapping (Boots et al., 2016).

Furthermore, knowledge levels often show considerable heterogeneity within the caregiver network. Factors such as higher levels of formal education and greater health literacy—defined as the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information needed to make appropriate health decisions—tend to be positively associated with better dementia knowledge. Conversely, the duration of the caregiving experience does not consistently predict accurate knowledge; long-term caregivers may still hold significant misconceptions. Even formal support workers may indicate a need for further training, particularly concerning the management of complex behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) and effective communication strategies for people with dementia.

This variability in knowledge and literacy poses a considerable challenge for fostering effective collaborative networks and for designing supportive eHealth tools. Health literacy is crucial for navigating complex care systems, communicating effectively with professionals, and making informed decisions, yet this capacity may be limited, especially among older adults who often form a significant part of the informal caregiver network. Lower health literacy has been linked to difficulties in managing chronic health conditions and less effective communication with healthcare providers. Communication difficulties are prevalent in healthcare encounters, with professionals sometimes using technical language or providing complex information without adequately verifying patient or caregiver understanding.

This interplay between dementia knowledge, health literacy, and communication experiences creates significant hurdles for the adoption and effective use of technology. Building useful eHealth "bridges" necessitates not only addressing general knowledge deficits but also actively countering specific misconceptions. Information delivered via these digital bridges must be accurate, evidence-based, appropriately targeted, and designed to dispel myths that hinder suitable care practices. Critically, considering the diverse educational backgrounds and health literacy levels within caregiver networks, these bridges must be designed with accessibility as a key consideration. This implies meticulous attention to aspects such as using plain language, ensuring clear information architecture, providing intuitive navigation, and possibly offering multiple formats (text, visual, audio/video) to prevent digital tools from becoming inaccessible barriers for individuals with lower literacy or limited digital proficiency. Designing eHealth applications for diverse care networks thus presents a considerable challenge: tools must be sufficiently intuitive for users with lower digital or health literacy while also offering the robust functionalities needed by more experienced or formal caregivers for effective coordination. A platform designed solely for simplicity might lack features for complex task management or detailed care planning, whereas a feature-rich platform might exclude less tech-savvy members, thereby undermining the goal of network-wide support (Christie et al., 2021).

Moreover, limited understanding of dementia or technology can diminish confidence in using eHealth tools and heighten concerns about privacy and data security. Combined with potentially negative prior communication experiences with the healthcare system that might undermine trust, caregivers could be hesitant to engage with eHealth platforms, particularly those connected to formal care providers or requiring the sharing of sensitive personal information. Consequently, eHealth solutions face the demanding task of being sufficiently simple for diverse users across the network, adequately robust for complex coordination tasks, and trustworthy enough to encourage adoption and facilitate the necessary sharing of information across the entire network. The way existing bridges attempt to resolve this tension between simplicity, functionality, and trust across heterogeneous user groups represents a key area for investigation in this review.

#### 1.1.3. Barriers to Effective Communication, Coordination, and Collaboration: Understanding Why Some Bridges Remain Difficult to Implement or Sustain

While the need for effective collaboration among the members of a dementia care network is apparent for ensuring continuity and quality of care, numerous obstacles frequently impede the cohesive functioning of these networks in practice. Such barriers span logistical, informational, emotional, interpersonal, and systemic domains, often interacting in complex ways to create significant challenges for caregivers and limiting the potential effectiveness of both traditional and digital coordination approaches. Understanding these multifaceted barriers is crucial for assessing why some eHealth interventions, conceptualized as "bridges," may fail to achieve sustained impact or remain challenging to implement effectively in real-world settings (Christie et al., 2018).

Accessing and sharing timely, pertinent information constitutes a major and persistent challenge. Secondary informal caregivers, friends, neighbours, and sometimes even formal providers involved in care may lack current details regarding the person's condition, changing needs, medication adjustments, scheduled appointments, or the established care plan. Obtaining this necessary information can prove difficult, often demanding proactive, time-consuming efforts from individuals who might already feel somewhat peripheral to the core care activities or hesitant to impose. Traditional methods for coordination, such as phone calls or informal notes, frequently prove inadequate for managing the complexities of distributed care networks, potentially leading to fragmented information, duplicated efforts, misunderstandings, and gaps in care. Additionally, the communication difficulties associated with dementia progression itself complicate interactions for all involved and necessitate specific communication adaptations and skills. eHealth communication and accompanying support systems aim to mitigate some of these issues (Dam et al., 2019), but their uptake and effectiveness vary. Care transitions, such as hospital discharge, pose particular communication challenges, especially for healthcare professionals concerning risk assessment and coordinating effectively with families and community services.

The emotional toll of dementia caregiving is considerable. Caregivers commonly report high levels of stress, burden, depressive symptoms, and anxiety. These strong emotions can function as significant barriers not only to caregiver well-being but also to seeking or accepting help from others within the network or from formal services. Feelings of guilt related to seeking external support or admitting difficulties are frequently reported. Pride or embarrassment may prevent individuals from acknowledging their need for assistance, while fear of judgment or fear for the future can contribute to social withdrawal and isolation. Witnessing the person's ongoing cognitive and functional decline also contributes significantly to emotional strain and grief. While psychosocial support tools delivered via eHealth aim to address these emotional needs (Boots et al., 2016), their integration into collaborative platforms and their impact on network dynamics require further investigation.

Trust forms a crucial foundation for effective collaboration, especially between informal caregivers and healthcare professionals, yet establishing and maintaining it involves a complex, ongoing process. A lack of trust, stemming from perceived lack of competence, poor communication, disrespect, or insufficient time dedicated to relationship building, can seriously inhibit open communication and genuine collaboration. Privacy considerations represent another significant barrier layer, particularly relevant for digital solutions that necessitate sharing sensitive personal information. Concerns regarding data security, unauthorized access to sensitive personal health information, and control over information flow are prevalent among caregivers and can deter the use of shared eHealth platforms (Christie et al., 2021).

The broader healthcare and social support systems themselves often present considerable obstacles to effective network collaboration. Service fragmentation, insufficient coordination between different providers and care settings (e.g., primary care, specialist care, social services), and poor integration between medical and social care pathways can lead to confusion, discontinuity in care, and increased burden for caregivers trying to navigate the system. The absence of a clearly designated care coordinator or navigator can exacerbate these problems. Healthcare professionals often report constraints related to time pressures, heavy workloads, and lack of specific reimbursement or organizational support for collaborative activities with informal caregivers or other professionals. Unclear roles, responsibilities, and complex procedures for accessing services further complicate navigation for families. Many caregivers lack awareness of potentially available support services or encounter significant difficulties in accessing them, even when eligible. Initiating contact with support systems can itself represent a substantial hurdle due to complexity or stigma. Even dedicated care coordination programs may face challenges in achieving seamless integration and communication across all network members. The potential for eHealth bridges to clarify responsibilities, streamline communication, or link caregivers to professionals and resources needs careful assessment in light of these systemic issues (Bartels et al., 2021).

These multifaceted barriers seldom occur in isolation; they frequently interact and compound each other, creating a challenging environment for collaborative care. This underscores that eHealth solutions intended as "bridges" cannot merely be technological fixes applied superficially. The successful implementation of eHealth interventions appears contingent not only on technological design but also on the extent to which they address the complex psychosocial and systemic barriers inherent in dementia care, such as issues of trust, emotional burden, and service fragmentation. A technically sophisticated communication platform, for instance, may fail if users lack trust in one another or the platform itself (Dam et al., 2017), or if it cannot effectively interface with disparate existing services. The effectiveness of a technological bridge may be significantly moderated by the pre-existing relational infrastructure within the network. If trust is low, communication patterns are poor, or roles are ill-defined, introducing technology might not resolve these issues and could potentially amplify dysfunction. Therefore, successful implementation likely necessitates attention to the network's relational health alongside technological deployment. To be effective and sustainable, eHealth bridges must be designed with sensitivity to the emotional context of caregiving, incorporate features that actively promote trust and manage privacy transparently, and ideally, assist caregivers in navigating or mitigating the effects of systemic fragmentation. Assessing how bridges were built, for instance through co-design processes involving diverse stakeholders (Christie et al., 2020), becomes relevant here to understand whether these crucial contextual factors were considered during development, potentially explaining why some bridges are more successfully implemented than others.

#### 1.1.4. The Needs of the Wider Care Circle: Building Bridges Beyond the Primary Caregiver

Support requirements within a dementia care network extend considerably beyond the individual designated as the primary caregiver. Secondary informal caregivers (other family, friends, neighbours), formal caregivers integrated into the home care arrangement (e.g., home care workers, community nurses), and others involved in the care circle have distinct yet often unmet needs that are critical for effective network functioning, the sustainability of the care arrangement, and the overall well-being of all involved, including the person with dementia. Understanding what various caregivers and other stakeholders want and need from better "bridges"—both technological and relational—is essential for designing truly supportive interventions (Dam et al., 2016).

Members of the wider network require timely and pertinent information to contribute effectively and appropriately. This includes updates on the person's health status and changing needs, practical tips for interaction or assistance, scheduling details for appointments or shared tasks, and guidance on communication strategies suitable for the stage of dementia. Access to such information enables meaningful contribution and prevents misunderstandings or conflicting approaches within the network. Beyond informational needs, emotional and social support are highly important for all members of the care circle. Secondary caregivers, who may feel less central but still significantly involved, often seek validation for their efforts, connection with peers facing similar situations, and understanding from others within and outside the network. Social support, encompassing both practical assistance (e.g., help with errands, respite) and psychosocial dimensions (e.g., empathy, encouragement), is vital for mitigating burden across the network and sustaining collective resilience over the long course of dementia (Christie et al., 2022).

Feeling appropriately involved in care processes and decision-making, commensurate with their role and relationship, is also crucial for members of the wider circle. Experiences of exclusion, being uninformed, or having their contributions unrecognized can lead to frustration, resentment, and conflict within the network. Practical coordination support, potentially facilitated through tools for task sharing, scheduling appointments or visits, maintaining shared care plans, or managing finances, is essential for distributing the care load more equitably and ensuring continuity and consistency of care. The question of how interconnected various support bridges are, or need to be, relates directly to this need for seamless, holistic support across different domains (e.g., linking communication tools with scheduling or information resources).

An inherent asymmetry often characterizes dementia care networks regarding needs and access to information and influence. Secondary caregivers may lack direct access to formal providers or updated information, often relying on the primary caregiver to act as a conduit or gatekeeper. The primary caregiver, frequently heavily burdened by direct care tasks and emotional stress (Dam et al., 2017), may struggle to effectively disseminate information, coordinate tasks, or manage communication flows across the network using traditional methods like phone calls or emails. Regrettably, many existing eHealth interventions appear to mirror this asymmetry, focusing primarily on the individual primary caregiver or the dyad (Christie et al., 2018), potentially reinforcing existing information silos rather than bridging them. This focus might stem partly from the tension between the primary caregiver's potential need for control or privacy regarding sensitive information and the wider network's requirement for access to facilitate shared care. Navigating this tension is a critical design consideration for network-supportive eHealth tools, requiring careful thought about information governance, permissions, and establishing clear network agreements. The potential role of the primary caregiver as a central hub, perhaps aided by technology like AI, is an area for exploration but must be balanced against the risk of further burdening that individual.

Consequently, to effectively harness the strengths and address the needs of the entire care network, eHealth interventions need to evolve beyond supporting only the primary caregiver. Digital tools must explicitly incorporate features designed to connect and support the whole circle of care. This requires a fundamental shift in design philosophy—moving away from merely supporting an individual user towards actively facilitating the processes of the network, such as communication, coordination, and shared support provision. Supporting the entire care circle necessitates network orchestration capabilities, managing varied roles, permissions, and information flows within potentially unified platforms. This implies functionalities such as role-based permissions allowing differential access to information, secure group communication channels, shared calendars and task lists with assignment capabilities, and easily accessible repositories for essential documents or information. Building effective digital support for dementia care networks entails constructing "multi-lane bridges" capable of handling diverse information flows, connecting multiple points concurrently, and empowering all members based on their specific roles, capabilities, and needs within the collaborative care process. This review will seek evidence of interventions designed with this network-centric philosophy (Bartels et al., 2021).

#### 1.1.5. eHealth as Potential Bridges: Current Landscape and Limitations

Electronic health (eHealth) applications, broadly defined (Christie et al., 2018), hold considerable potential for addressing many of the traditional barriers encountered within dementia care networks. They offer mechanisms to overcome geographical distances between network members, enable asynchronous or synchronous communication, potentially facilitate more efficient and transparent information sharing, support collaborative task management (Dam et al., 2019), and provide platforms for developing and accessing shared care plans. Digital tools can make the often "invisible work" of care coordination more visible, structured, and manageable. Furthermore, access to curated online information, educational resources, and peer support forums can empower caregivers with knowledge and reduce feelings of isolation.

Research, predominantly conducted in controlled contexts such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), suggests positive outcomes associated with various eHealth interventions for dementia caregivers. These include improvements in caregiver self-efficacy, dementia-related knowledge, coping skills, and psychological well-being (e.g., reduced symptoms of depression, anxiety, or stress (Boots et al., 2016)), and sometimes reduced caregiver burden or distress. Features allowing for tailored information delivery and decision support appear particularly valued by caregivers. Multi-component interventions, often combining educational modules with interactive elements like coaching or peer support, may be more effective than single-component approaches. Related technologies like telehealth for integrated care and personal health records (PHRs) for self-management also show promise in adjacent areas, suggesting potential for integration. However, understanding how effective these various digital bridges are in supporting multi-caregiver collaboration and improving network-level outcomes in real-world practice remains a critical question. It is noteworthy that quantitative results from RCTs sometimes show limited or no significant effects on primary well-being outcomes, while qualitative data from the same studies often highlight user appreciation for process improvements like better coordination or communication. This suggests that conventional effectiveness metrics might not fully capture the perceived value of these tools for network functioning (Christie et al., 2020).

Despite the documented potential and positive findings from efficacy trials, a significant gap persists between research evidence and the successful implementation, adoption, and sustained use of eHealth interventions in routine dementia care practice. Many interventions shown to be effective in research settings fail to transition beyond pilot phases or achieve widespread uptake, falling into a "pilot-to-practice chasm" or "valley of death" (Christie et al., 2018). The reasons for this are numerous and complex, involving characteristics of the technology, the users, the implementing organizations, and the broader context. The fundamental question of whether caregivers and other stakeholders actually want to use the proposed bridges, or perceive them as beneficial and usable within their daily lives and existing routines, is central to this gap.

The landscape of available tools includes not only academically developed and evaluated interventions but also a growing number of commercially available applications. These commercial tools are often marketed directly to caregivers and may promise support for coordination or communication. However, they frequently lack rigorous, independent evaluation of their effectiveness, usability, or impact on network collaboration. Therefore, while mapping these commercial "bridges" is essential for understanding the full spectrum of tools available to caregivers, the search strategy for these commercial projects within this review will primarily focus on identifying what bridges are built and marketed for multi-caregiver support (e.g., via app stores, company websites). Data regarding their development process, formal evaluation, effectiveness, or long-term sustainability are often unavailable in the public domain and thus cannot be systematically assessed in the same manner as academic interventions. Nonetheless, characterizing their existence and purported functions is crucial for a comprehensive map of the field.

Systematic reviews have identified a wide array of barriers hindering the implementation of eHealth interventions more broadly. These include usability issues, excessive complexity, lack of user-friendliness (especially for older users or those with low digital literacy (Dam et al., 2017)), poor technical design or inadequate support, technical problems and unreliability, costs associated with development, maintenance, or access (Christie et al., 2021), difficulties integrating eHealth tools into existing clinical workflows and electronic health records (EHRs) (Bartels et al., 2021), insufficient training and ongoing support for both caregivers and professionals (Dam et al., 2019), significant privacy and security concerns often linked to a lack of trust in the technology or providers (Christie et al., 2020), lack of organizational readiness, leadership support, or a clear implementation strategy (Christie et al., 2018), and caregiver preference for traditional methods or general apprehension about using technology.

A critical limitation, particularly relevant to this review, is that many existing eHealth applications, especially those available commercially, often lack robust features specifically designed for multi-user network collaboration. They frequently focus instead on the individual primary caregiver or the caregiver-patient dyad (Christie et al., 2018), thereby failing to adequately address the collaborative needs of the wider care circle. Consequently, the specific effectiveness of eHealth for enhancing communication, coordination, and collaboration among diverse caregivers remains relatively under-investigated in real-world practice settings.

This difficulty in translating research into practice appears partly driven by a fundamental mismatch between research priorities (often focused on demonstrating efficacy under ideal conditions) and the complex realities of implementation. There is often a failure within the research and development process itself to adequately consider implementation from the outset. If development prioritizes efficacy in controlled trials without concurrently addressing usability for diverse end-users (Boots et al., 2016), integration into complex clinical and home workflows (Bartels et al., 2021), building user trust (Dam et al., 2016), ensuring cultural appropriateness, and planning for long-term technical and user support, the resulting intervention is often ill-prepared for real-world adoption, regardless of its demonstrated potential in a trial setting. Issues critical for real-world adoption – usability for diverse users, workflow integration, cost-effectiveness, organizational buy-in, long-term support – are frequently secondary considerations. Implementation science frameworks, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) (Christie et al., 2020), which highlight the need to consider the interplay between the intervention, the individuals involved, the inner and outer organizational settings, and the implementation process itself (Christie et al., 2021), are often under-addressed or applied late in eHealth dementia research. Commercial applications, driven by different priorities, may prioritize marketability over rigorously evaluated usability or sophisticated collaboration features. The particular context of dementia caregiver support likely exacerbates these general eHealth implementation challenges. Designing for potentially less digitally literate older adults (Dam et al., 2017), addressing the complexities of network interactions (Christie et al., 2022), navigating fragmented health and social care systems (Dam et al., 2019), and securing sustainable funding beyond research grants create a confluence of barriers making the journey across the "valley of death" particularly arduous for these interventions. As a result, many potential eHealth "bridges" might be developed but remain largely "uncrossed" in practice because they are perceived as too difficult to use, are poorly integrated into existing care pathways, are not trusted by users, or lack the necessary organizational and technical support for sustainability. Therefore, this scoping review must investigate not only what bridges exist but also why certain tools are, or are not, successfully implemented, adopted, and sustained over time, by examining reported implementation determinants. This includes exploring how bridges have evolved since their initial conception and if they are still standing and available for use.

### 1.2. Review Questions and Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review is to systematically map the landscape of eHealth applications designed to support communication, coordination, and collaboration (CCC) and provide social or psychosocial support among diverse caregivers (both formal and informal) involved in the care of persons with dementia residing at home. Utilizing the metaphor of "bridges" to conceptualize these technologies, the review will specifically address the following questions:

1. **What types of bridges have been built?** What eHealth applications have been developed to support CCC and social/psychosocial support among diverse caregivers of persons with dementia living at home? What features, functionalities, and purposes characterize these applications?

2. **Who are the bridges built for?** Which caregiver network members (e.g., primary informal caregivers, secondary caregivers, formal caregivers, others) do these applications target and aim to connect?

3. **How were the bridges built?** What processes (e.g., co-design, user involvement, theoretical foundations) and features (e.g., technology platform, interface design, integration capabilities) were employed in the development of these applications?

4. **How have the bridges been evaluated?** What methodological approaches and outcome measures have been employed to assess these applications? What evidence exists regarding their effectiveness for supporting CCC among diverse caregivers or improving outcomes for caregivers, care recipients, or the care network as a whole?

5. **What do users think of the bridges?** What are the perspectives, preferences, and experiences of diverse stakeholders (different caregiver types, care recipients, healthcare providers) regarding these applications?

6. **How have bridges evolved, and are they still standing?** How have these eHealth applications evolved since their initial development? Are they still available, maintained, and used in practice, or have they been discontinued? What factors influence their sustainability over time?

7. **Why are some bridges still too far?** What factors (technological, individual, interpersonal, organizational, contextual) facilitate or hinder the implementation, adoption, and sustained use of these applications in real-world practice?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Study Design

This scoping review will be conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Dam et al., 2019). The review will be guided by a prespecified protocol to ensure methodological rigor and transparency.

### 2.2. Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria for inclusion have been established based on the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework as follows:

#### 2.2.1. Population

The review will include studies involving formal and informal caregivers (e.g., family members, friends, neighbors, home care workers, healthcare professionals) of persons with dementia living at home. Studies focusing exclusively on caregivers of institutionalized persons with dementia will be excluded.

#### 2.2.2. Concept

The review will focus on eHealth applications explicitly designed to support communication, coordination, and collaboration (CCC) and/or social or psychosocial support among multiple caregivers involved in the care of persons with dementia. These may include, but are not limited to, digital platforms, web-based applications, mobile applications, telehealth systems, or other technology-enabled tools that aim to facilitate information sharing, care planning, task coordination, problem-solving, decision-making, emotional support, or relationship-building within caregiver networks (Christie et al., 2022).

Studies will be included if they meet at least one of the following criteria:
- The eHealth application is explicitly designed to connect or support collaboration between multiple caregivers (e.g., facilitating communication between primary and secondary caregivers, between family caregivers and professional care providers).
- The eHealth application, while not originally designed specifically for multiple caregivers, is used in practice to support care coordination among diverse network members, and this use is described in the study.

Studies focusing exclusively on the following will be excluded:
- Technologies designed solely to assist the person with dementia without any component aimed at caregiver support or collaboration.
- Technologies aimed exclusively at supporting a single primary caregiver without any functionality for connecting with other caregivers or care providers.
- Non-digital interventions (e.g., paper-based coordination tools, in-person support groups).

#### 2.2.3. Context

The review will consider studies conducted in any geographical or healthcare setting, provided they involve caregivers of persons with dementia living in home or community settings (not permanently institutionalized). No restrictions will be placed on the country of origin, cultural context, or healthcare system.

#### 2.2.4. Types of Sources

The review will include a wide range of publication types to ensure comprehensive coverage of the field:
- Peer-reviewed research articles reporting empirical studies (qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods)
- Review articles (systematic, scoping, narrative) addressing the topic
- Protocol papers describing the development or evaluation of relevant eHealth applications
- Grey literature, including reports, white papers, conference proceedings, dissertations, and theses
- Descriptions of commercially available applications from app stores or provider websites

Given the rapidly evolving nature of technology, publications from January 2000 onwards will be considered. Only publications in English will be included due to resource constraints.

### 2.3. Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy will be developed in consultation with an information specialist to ensure adequate coverage of both the academic literature and relevant grey literature sources.

#### 2.3.1. Database Searches

Systematic searches will be conducted in the following electronic databases:
- PubMed/MEDLINE
- Embase
- PsycINFO
- CINAHL
- Web of Science Core Collection
- Scopus
- ACM Digital Library
- IEEE Xplore

Search terms will include controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) and free-text terms covering three main concepts:
1. Dementia (e.g., "dementia," "Alzheimer's disease," "cognitive impairment")
2. Caregivers (e.g., "caregiver," "carer," "family member," "care network," "care team," "informal care," "formal care," "home care worker")
3. eHealth for communication, coordination, and collaboration (e.g., "eHealth," "digital health," "mHealth," "telehealth," "information technology," "communication," "coordination," "collaboration," "support," "network")

The search strategy will be peer-reviewed by a second information specialist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist (Christie et al., 2020). A sample search strategy for PubMed is presented in Appendix A.

#### 2.3.2. Grey Literature Searches

To identify commercially available applications and other non-indexed relevant resources, the following sources will be searched:
- App stores (Apple App Store, Google Play Store)
- Major eHealth platform provider websites
- Research project websites and databases (e.g., EU-funded project repositories, NIH Reporter)
- Relevant organizational websites (e.g., Alzheimer's associations, caregiver support organizations)
- Conference proceedings
- OpenGrey, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses

#### 2.3.3. Supplementary Search Methods

To enhance comprehensiveness, the following supplementary search methods will be employed:
- Forward and backward citation searching of included studies
- Hand-searching of key journals
- Contacting key researchers in the field for information about unpublished or ongoing studies
- Social media searches (e.g., Twitter, LinkedIn) using relevant hashtags

### 2.4. Study Selection

Study selection will proceed in two stages, with all decisions recorded in a PRISMA flow diagram.

#### 2.4.1. Title and Abstract Screening

Two reviewers will independently screen all titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Articles will be coded as "include," "exclude," or "uncertain." Disagreements will be resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if necessary. Articles coded as "include" or "uncertain" will progress to full-text review.

#### 2.4.2. Full-Text Review

Two reviewers will independently assess the full texts of potentially eligible articles against the detailed inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion will be recorded. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

### 2.5. Data Extraction

A structured data charting form will be developed and piloted on a sample of 5-10 included studies to ensure it captures all relevant information and is applied consistently. Two reviewers will independently extract data from the first 10 included studies to verify the reliability of the extraction process. Thereafter, data extraction will be conducted by one reviewer and checked by a second.

The data charting form will include the following categories:

1. **Source characteristics**: Author(s), year of publication, country, publication type, funding source
2. **Bridge type and features**: Application name, technology platform, purpose, target users, functionalities, theoretical underpinnings, connectivity features
3. **Development methodology**: Design approach, user involvement, co-design processes, development timeline
4. **Evaluation approach**: Study design, sample characteristics, evaluation methods, outcome measures, follow-up period
5. **Outcomes and effectiveness**: Main findings, reported effects on communication, coordination, collaboration, psychosocial outcomes, user satisfaction and engagement
6. **Stakeholder views**: Perspectives of different user groups, perceived benefits, challenges, suggestions for improvement
7. **Evolution and sustainability**: Updates, modifications, current availability, maintenance approach, longevity
8. **Implementation factors**: Reported facilitators and barriers to implementation, adoption, and sustained use, including technological, individual, organizational, and contextual factors

For commercial applications identified through grey literature, available information may be limited to descriptive features, target users, and purported functionalities, which will be duly noted.

### 2.6. Quality Assessment

As is common in scoping reviews, formal quality assessment of included studies will not be performed. However, to enhance the interpretability of findings, basic methodological characteristics of empirical studies (e.g., study design, sample size, data collection methods) will be extracted and reported as part of the results.

### 2.7. Data Synthesis and Presentation

Findings will be synthesized using a narrative approach structured around the review questions. The "bridges" metaphor will be employed as an organizing framework to present and interpret the results. Visual representations, including tables, charts, and conceptual diagrams, will be used to map the identified applications according to their features, target users, evaluation approaches, and reported effectiveness.

For each review question, we will analyze patterns, trends, and gaps in the evidence, considering variations across different types of eHealth applications, target populations, and contexts. The synthesis will pay particular attention to factors that appear to influence the successful implementation and sustainability of these digital "bridges" in real-world practice settings, thereby addressing the central question of why some bridges remain "too far."

## 3. Discussion

This scoping review protocol outlines a comprehensive approach to mapping the landscape of eHealth applications designed to support communication, coordination, and collaboration among diverse caregivers of persons with dementia living at home. By conceptualizing these technologies as "bridges," the review aims to provide insights not only into what digital solutions exist and how they function but also into factors that influence whether these bridges successfully connect caregivers and sustain their utility over time.

The findings from this review are expected to make several important contributions. First, they will provide a comprehensive overview of existing eHealth applications in this field, categorizing them according to their features, functionalities, and target users. This mapping will help identify patterns, trends, and gaps in the current landscape. Second, the review will synthesize evidence regarding the effectiveness of these applications for supporting caregiver networks, highlighting approaches and features associated with positive outcomes. Third, by examining development methodologies and stakeholder perspectives, the review will offer insights into best practices for designing user-centered, network-oriented eHealth tools. Fourth, the analysis of implementation factors will illuminate barriers and facilitators to the successful adoption and sustained use of these technologies in real-world settings, providing guidance for future implementation efforts (Bartels et al., 2021).

The findings are expected to be of value to multiple stakeholders. For researchers and technology developers, the review will identify research gaps, methodological considerations, and design principles to inform future work. For healthcare providers and organizations, it will offer insights into selecting and implementing appropriate eHealth solutions to support care networks. For policymakers, the findings regarding implementation challenges and sustainability factors may inform policies and funding decisions related to digital health infrastructure and caregiver support programs. Ultimately, the goal is to contribute to the development and implementation of more effective, sustainable "bridges" that meaningfully support the complex networks of care surrounding persons with dementia in community settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The review focuses exclusively on English-language publications, potentially missing relevant work in other languages. The rapid evolution of technology means that some applications may become outdated during the review process. Information about commercial applications may be limited by what is publicly available, and the actual effectiveness of these tools may not be well-documented. Despite these limitations, the comprehensive approach outlined in this protocol, incorporating both academic and grey literature sources, is designed to provide the most complete picture possible of this complex and evolving field.
